Investigate once, dispatch many

Conducting an entire epic with agents, and what the first real run taught me.

TL;DR

What it isInstead of refining story by story, you investigate once at the scope of the whole epic and inject that understanding into every story. A conductor that dispatches the skills you already have, without reimplementing any of them.

WhyIsolated refinement makes the same ground get reinvestigated N times, and each pass reaches a slightly different conclusion.

What I did not expectThe pilot's real deliverable was not the epic. It was the list of defects in the tool itself.

The concept

An epic is not a bunch of independent stories.

They share the same ground: the same repos, the same schema, the same cross-cutting decisions. When you refine them one at a time, each refinement reinvestigates that ground from zero — and reaches a conclusion slightly different from the last. Then you discover on the third story that the second assumed something else.

The conductor's idea is to move the investigation up a level. One grounding pass at the epic's scope, cross-cutting decisions locked there, and only then is each story specified already seeded with that. Nothing is reinvestigated. The single-item skills stay the same, with the same gates — the conductor just calls them in the right order, with the right context.

The part that changed my mind

Code beats the document. Literally, not as a slogan.

The epic's refinement wiki I used as a guinea pig was good. Well written, detailed, and it described a state of the code that no longer existed.

Grounding runs exploration agents in parallel, one per repository, and every claim in the wiki has to be confirmed against the development branch, with file:line.

It has a sibling on the frontend: the screen is the spec. Layout, states and copy do not survive a written description, so grounding looks at a real screenshot of the target screen — usually already attached to the story itself. A design tool gives exact measurements to implement pixel-faithfully, but for understanding and estimating the screenshot wins: no access friction, the designer's annotations already on it, and showing the chosen state instead of a file full of drafts.

And the open questions: before pinging the PO, try answering them yourself. In my run, of the ones I was about to escalate, one fell straight out of the product rules already written and another fell out of the code — it was a different test framework than the task description claimed. The answer is usually already written somewhere.

Sequencing

The waves are not stored anywhere.

Sequence is not state. Each run the conductor recomputes: the current wave is every uncompleted task whose predecessors are ready. That makes the thing resumable — you can stop halfway, the board is the source of truth, and on return it recomputes.

The detail that makes it work: predecessor ready means merged or with an open PR to stack on. If you require a merge, every wave waits for a human and the parallelism evaporates. This is where stacked PRs stop being a preference and become a requirement.

Autonomy

Gates in front, not in the middle.

This is not autopilot, and the difference is in where the gates sit. They are front-loaded: confirm what will be done, approve the plan, confirm the hours, and review in batch once all PRs are open. Between plan approval and "all PRs open", it runs on its own — and only interrupts on a real blocker.

A gate between every step tires people out and turns into a rubber stamp: you approve without reading because you have approved seventeen times today. A gate in the right place, you read.

Autonomy goes up to the open PR. Who decides what merges is a person.

In my pilot this was tested the right way: the human review of the frontend stack came back approved, but with two real blockers the agent had let through. The best one — two eslint-disable in one PR, violating the squad's zero-suppression rule. "Lint passed" is not "no suppression": suppression is how you make lint pass.

Grounding in delivery

What only shows up when you actually run it.

One thing I did not expect: driving delivery to a working visual demo found what no spec read would have found.

An identity bug that only failed on the real write, and failed on both possible sides — the provisional value violated the foreign key, and the only real principal was exactly what the validation rejected. And a worker crash that was not code: it was a directory that had never been shared with the VM.

None of that was in any story or wiki page. It appeared because someone cloned the repos, brought the stack up and ran the thing. The spec tells you the plan; grounding tells you the state.

The pilot

The real deliverable was not the epic.

Running this for the first time on a real epic exposed eight concrete failures in the conductor itself and in the skills it dispatches:

01

Never assigned the stories

Did not assign the in-scope stories to whoever was touching them, nor move the epic to In Progress.

02

Reported child tasks halfway

Showed they existed, but not who owned them, whether they were estimated, or their refinement state.

03

Asked for design that was already attached

The stories already carried annotated screenshots of the prototype. Look at the attachments first.

04

Could not upload images to the wiki

And the existing workaround orphaned all eight images silently, which is worse than failing.

Six of the eight were fixed the same day, in three themed PRs. The tool improved before the epic finished, and that ended up being worth more than the epic.

If you are going to try orchestration like this, it is what I recommend expecting from the first run: it is not about delivering faster, it is about finding out where your process lies to you.

How to reuse without committing

The two halves separate, and the first one is free.

Just the grounding, writing nothing anywhere: understand an epic, estimate, build a handover, prepare a demo. Run the method, drop the result into a local doc, and no card is touched. Cheap, no board risk, and it still fixes the wiki. The pocket rule is to trust the wiki's intent and distrust its implementation claims.

The orchestration half only after confirming the board is not ahead of the wiki — if the stories are already refined and already have subtasks, you recreate work that exists.

If you take a single phrase: investigating once is the win. Investigate at the epic's scope, specify each story seeded with it, and let the code — not the document — tell you what actually exists.
